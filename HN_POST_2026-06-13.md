**Title**: MCP governance: 28 hives, 13 frameworks, 1 HMAC chain

**Body**:

We open-sourced a 28-hive compliance mesh for the Model Context Protocol. Three things we learned shipping it that weren't obvious when we started.

**1. The "trust boundary" in MCP is the tool call, not the server.**

Most MCP servers today are monolithic: the server, the tools, the data, and the audit trail all live in the same process. That fails open the moment you have to answer the question "what tool was called, by whom, against what data, with what attestation?" — which is the question every AI compliance regime (EU AI Act Article 12, China Generative AI Interim Measures Article 15, Colorado ADMT §6-1-1705) asks.

We split this into two layers:
- A **stateless streamable-HTTP gateway** (the keystone, `meok-compliance-gateway`) that authenticates, routes, signs, and emits the audit event.
- A fleet of **single-purpose MCP servers** (28 of them, one per AI domain) that own their own data and tools but cannot emit an audit event without the gateway.

The gateway is **stateless by design** — no session memory, no client tracking. It uses HMAC-SHA256 (FIPS 180-4) over `(timestamp, tool, caller, input_hash, output_hash)` with a per-deployment attestation key resolved from a secret manager (AWS Secrets Manager, GCP Secret Manager, HashiCorp Vault, or stdlib `keyring` with chmod 600 fallback). The chain is **append-only**, indexed by `(deployment, tool, window)`, and verifiable by any third party with the public key.

This was driven by the **MCP 2026-07-28 spec freeze** ([draft](https://modelcontextprotocol.io)) which deprecates stateful session handling. Anything we built for the gateway had to work across both pre- and post-freeze clients — which is why we didn't reach for WebSockets or session cookies.

**2. Multi-framework governance is a substrate problem, not a content problem.**

The EU AI Act has 410 articles. China's Interim Measures have 5 obligations + 12 prohibited-content categories. Colorado ADMT has 16 impact-assessment sections. ISO 42001, SOC 2, NIST AI RMF, and DORA add another ~300 controls. That's ~1,000 discrete compliance checks across 8-13 frameworks per AI system.

The naive approach is "one prompt per framework, with a different system prompt for each." That doesn't work: the same AI system has the **same data**, **same model**, **same deployment context** — re-prompting the same LLM to re-evaluate the same evidence is wasteful and produces drift.

Our answer is **a single classification + evidence-assembly engine** that emits per-framework reports from one shared `SystemState`. The keystone's 13-framework coverage (EU AI Act, GDPR, HIPAA, DORA, NIS2, CRA, CSRD, ESG, ISO 42001, ISO 27001, SOC 2, NIST AI RMF, supply-chain) shares the same `classify_annex_iii`-style back-end and the same evidence corpus. New frameworks (China, Colorado, ETSI) drop in as new tool surfaces, not new stacks.

**3. The x402 paywall is the smallest bridge from "open source" to "revenue."**

We didn't want to gate source code behind a SaaS login. The keystone is MIT-licensed. But we needed to fund the engineering, and the gap between "open source" and "$50K/yr enterprise contract" is too wide to cross with a Stripe checkout.

[Coinbase's x402 protocol](https://github.com/coinbase/x402) lets us charge per tool call (USDC, $0.001-$50 per call) without forcing account creation. We have 8 `@paywalled` tools live on the keystone today. Customers top up a USDC wallet, the gateway enforces a 402 Payment Required response when the balance is exhausted, and the tool returns a result. The reconciliation is one webhook.

This works because **the per-call cost of an MCP tool is genuinely small** (a few cents of inference + a few cents of storage + a few cents of bandwidth). At $0.05/call, a developer doing 1,000 calls/month pays $50/month. A buyer doing 1,000,000 calls/month (an enterprise, basically) pays $50,000/month — and we'd rather charge that as 1,000,000 x $0.05 than as "$50K/yr, no per-seat math, no overage logic." The unit economics line up.

**What's open-sourced today (all MIT):**

- `meok-compliance-gateway` — the stateless streamable-HTTP gateway, HMAC chain, x402 paywall
- 13 governance-framework MCPs (one per framework)
- 4 P0-build MCPs in the launch wave (China, EU AI Act high-risk classifier, ETSI CABCA continuous-conformity, Colorado ADMT)
- 4 flagship MCPs (COBOL bridge, proof attestation, AI agent memory, openMCP cross-post)
- 16 vertical/industry MCPs (UK construction, AGI safety, security, bias, privacy, etc.)
- 28 GEO/AEO landing pages (llms.txt, index.html, sitemap.xml, JSON-LD FAQ schema) — the public surface

**OpenSSF Scorecard**: 81.6/100 on the keystone. The full 14-repo matrix is in `FLEET_SCORE.md` (in the repo root). The remediation work — cosign keyless signing, SBOM, Trivy, SLSA provenance, branch protection, CodeQL, dependency update tooling — is in `openssf-scorecard-remediation-2026-06-06.md`.

**What we got wrong, and would do differently:**

- We initially tried to do the consumer-facing side (the customer dashboards) in the same repo. We split that into `meok.ai` (the B2B portal) and `csoai.org` (the governance institution) about 6 weeks in. Would have done that split on day 1.
- The `memoria` Git-for-memory integration is the right idea but the wrong substrate — it's Rust, and the integration cost is high for marginal benefit at our scale. We're running an in-memory agent memory for now and revisiting Memoria in Q4 2026.
- The "monolithic 25-domain portfolio" was over-scoped. We've since marked 4 of the 25 domains as `flip` candidates (sell to a domain buyer) and 1 as `expire` (typo squat, let it lapse). The remaining 20 are the active fleet.
